(1-NN) per point; region = chord-relative distance to nearest airfoil-surface
point. Streamwise span x_extent = 2*POS_SCALE[0] = 2.25; chord_x median 1.214.

## Headline numbers

- **Dyadic peaks confirmed.** The pooled histogram is multi-modal with discrete
  spikes (mesh is dyadically refined). The far field collapses onto a single
  coarse level (0.0156); finer levels stack toward the surface.
- **Strong non-uniformity.** Far/near median spacing contrast ≈ **5.7×**.
  Most points live near the surface: the near band (<0.1 chord) holds 5.4M of
  8.0M pooled points; surface itself 1.3M.
- **The points out-resolve every grid near the surface.** Near-surface median
  spacing 0.0028 implies an equivalent uniform N_x ≈ **800** to match
  (≈400 at Nyquist); the surface band (0.0016) implies N_x ≈ 1400. Even the
  finest rung (256³, Δx=0.0088) is **3–5× coarser** than the near-surface
  spacing. All four grid pitches sit to the *right* of (coarser than) the
  surface/near peaks in the figure.
- **The far field is the opposite.** Far-field spacing 0.0156 sits between the
  128³ (0.0176) and 256³ (0.0088) pitches — there the grid roughly matches or
  slightly over-samples the cloud.

## How this fits the substrate / resolution-axis thesis (CORRECTED framing)

The hard, high-variance "Wake" region is NOT a coarse far-downstream wake — per
`receptive_field_physics/report.md` it is a thin slab that HUGS the airfoil
surface (~1.15 chord in x, only ~0.12 chord downstream). `spacing_by_variance.py`
confirms it directly: p90-variance ("hot") points have median 1-NN spacing
**0.0022 — 3.9× FINER than the 256³ grid pitch (0.0088)**; 98% of wake points
are spaced finer than even the finest grid rung. So the hard region is the
*finest-sampled* region, finer than every grid.

This RETIRES the literal "graph is critically sampled at the signal's Nyquist /
can't represent high frequencies." Where it matters, the cloud out-resolves
every grid. The Nyquist-ceiling metaphor is wrong in the hot region.

What the data forces instead (operator claim, not representation claim):
- The grid keeps improving 64³→256³ (Wake 0.2135→0.1594) **while still 4× coarser
  than the cloud** in the very region it improves. It never catches the cloud's
  native density and still gains.
- The graph has STRICTLY MORE input fidelity in the hot region (no bin-average,
  finer sampling) and still loses to the coarser grid.
- => the limiter is NOT sampling density or representational bandwidth (graph
  wins both). It is the **operator / substrate type** + a **resolution knob**:
  a regular, weight-shared, equivariant conv lattice is a better computational
  fabric for this field→field map than fixed irregular message-passing — even at
  lower sampling density — and it has a capacity/resolution axis (add cells,
  trade compute for substrate, independent of I/O) the graph structurally lacks.
  Refinement pays because more cells = more headroom for the *operator* to carry
  the transformed high-freq field, not because the grid is finally sampling the
  input finely enough (it isn't, even at 256³).
- The `0.063` coincidence (graph C ≈ 64³ voxel) re-reads as a shared
  *operator-quality* floor (good per-point features + global context on a
  baseline substrate), not a shared sampling Nyquist — and only the grid has a
  dial past it.
- Still resolution-limited at 256³ near the surface (grid Δx=0.0088 vs cloud
  0.0022 → 4× headroom remaining before the grid even matches the cloud) — the
  ladder is substrate-limited, not cloud-limited.

## Axis-wise spacing + dense-population fraction (point_spacing_axiswise.py)

**(A) Sampling is ISOTROPIC — not denser along the flow (x) axis.** Per-axis
nearest-neighbour spacing (dominant-component binning over k=16), 80 samples:

| axis | p25 | p50 | p75 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| x (streamwise) | 0.0020 | 0.0039 | 0.0078 |
| y (spanwise) | 0.0020 | 0.0038 | 0.0078 |
| z (vertical) | 0.0020 | 0.0038 | 0.0078 |

x:y:z = **1.02 : 0.99 : 1.00**; identical dyadic peaks across all three axes.
The mesh refines isotropically (octree-style — a cube splits into 8 equal
sub-cubes, shrinking spacing equally in every axis). The non-uniformity is
purely *spatial* (near-surface dense → far-field sparse), not *directional*.
NB the **grid** is anisotropic by modeling choice (Δx:Δy:Δz = 0.88:0.69:1.00,
finest in y, coarsest in z — from N×N/2×N/2 over the 2.25×0.878×1.281 box), so
the worst grid-vs-cloud pitch mismatch is along z. The cloud's own sampling
carries no axis preference.

**(B) The dense region is most of the cloud.** Fraction of points by
chord-relative distance to the airfoil surface:

| within | fraction |
|---|---:|
| on surface | 16.2% |
| < 0.05 chord | 57.5% |
| < 0.1 chord | 67.1% |
| < 0.25 chord | 77.4% |
| < 0.5 chord | 81.3% |
| ≥ 0.5 chord (far field) | **2.6%** |

By isotropic 1-NN spacing: median point 0.0028; **78.0% of all points are
finer-spaced than the 256³ grid pitch**, 91.6% finer than 128³, 98.6% finer
than 64³. The fine/dense population (spacing ≲ 0.008) is ~75–80% of the cloud;
the coarse far-field level (~0.0156) is only the top ~10–15% (1-NN percentiles
p50=0.0028, p75=0.0078, p90=0.0156). So ~57% of all points sit within 0.05
chord of the surface and only 2.6% are far field — the dense near-airfoil
region *is* the cloud. Strengthens the operator/dial reading: the grid is
coarser than the cloud for ~80% of points even at 256³ and still wins.

## Caveats

- 1-NN distance is a local point-spacing proxy, not a true spectral Nyquist;
  the "equivalent N_x" figures are order-of-magnitude, not exact.
- Region bands are chord-relative (distance to nearest surface point); the
  surface band is exactly `idcs_airfoil`, so it includes both sides of thin
  trailing edges where 1-NN can be very small (p05=0.0001).
- Anisotropic grids (x:y:z = 2:1:1 cells over a 2.25:0.88:1.28 box) — Δx is the
  streamwise pitch only; y/z pitches differ. Spacing here is isotropic 3-D 1-NN.
- 80-sample pool; re-run with `--all` for the full 810-file distribution
  (numbers were stable across the 20-sample checkpoints).
- Per-axis spacing uses dominant-component binning (assign each of k=16
  neighbours to the axis of its largest |Δ| component, take the nearest per
  axis). It would *detect* anisotropy if present (the finer axis would show a
  smaller min); the near-identical x/y/z medians are genuine isotropy, not a
  method artefact. Points with no neighbour dominant in a given axis (rare at
  k=16) are dropped for that axis.
